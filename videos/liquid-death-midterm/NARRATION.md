# Narration script: "Liquid Death: How Marketing Made Water Cool"

Live narration for the 6:25 video. Pace: about 130 words per minute, calm and
deliberate. **[cue]** marks the on-screen moment to wait for; *(pause)* means stop
talking and let the screen work. Word counts leave silence for the visual beats.

Tip: rehearse once with the video. If you fall behind, skip the sentences marked ◇.
They are supporting detail, and the argument still holds without them.

---

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:35)

**[0:03, "IT'S JUST WATER." types on]** *(stay silent)*

**[0:08, the plain bottle]**
This is how most of us picture bottled water: clear, clean, simple. A product we
buy for one reason, which is that we are thirsty.

**[0:14, cut to the black can]** *(pause, let the sound land)*

**[0:24, "THEN WHY DOES IT LOOK LIKE THIS?"]**
So why does this water look like a can of beer at a metal concert?

**[0:29, the sub-line appears]**
That is my question today: how can marketing make an ordinary product feel
culturally different?

## 2 · Before the brand (0:35–1:15)

**[0:36, the shelf builds]**
Start with the category. Before Liquid Death, the need for water was already fully
established. Nobody had to be convinced that hydration matters.

**[0:45, HYDRATION]** Water brands met that functional need,
**[0:50, PRICE]** competed on price,
**[0:55, AVAILABILITY]** on where you could buy them,
**[1:00, PACKAGING]** and on how they looked on the shelf.

**[1:05, the caption]**
So the need already existed. What brands competed for was attention. To be fair,
many water brands already used distinctive marketing. Liquid Death did not
invent branding in this category. ◇ This slide is a simplified view.

## 3 · Introducing Liquid Death (1:15–2:00)

**[1:17, the spotlight opens]**
Enter Liquid Death.

**[1:23, the close-ups]**
A tall aluminum can. Heavy-metal typography. A skull. Every visual cue is borrowed
from places water usually never goes: beer, energy drinks, punk and metal culture.

**[1:35, the founder card]**
The brand was founded by Mike Cessario, a former advertising creative, and it sold
its first cans in 2019, according to BeverageDaily and TechCrunch.

**[1:43, the two panels]**
Look at the contrast. Conventional water branding promises purity, nature and
freshness. Liquid Death promises rebellion, humor and attitude.

**[1:52, the analysis line]**
My reading, and I want to flag it as interpretation: their main differentiation
was not a new liquid. It was a new identity built around an old one.

## 4 · How the marketing works (2:00–3:00)

**[2:04, the four panels]**
How does that identity get built? I see four mechanisms.

**[2:05, VISUAL DIFFERENTIATION]**
First, visual differentiation. On a shelf of clear bottles, a black tallboy is
impossible to miss.

**[2:14, MURDER YOUR THIRST]**
Second, shock and humor. "Murder your thirst" is the company's own slogan, and it
is deliberately absurd for a product that is just water.

**[2:24, the montage]**
Third, entertainment-first content. The ads are made to be watched and shared:
a 2022 regional Super Bowl spot where kids "chug" tallboys, ending with "Don't be
scared. It's just water"; a 2020 heavy-metal album built from real hate comments;
a 2022 Halloween collaboration with Martha Stewart; and a 2023 blind taste test
against back sweat. ◇ In a 2024 Fortune interview, Cessario described this as
making fun of extreme marketing.

**[2:40, IDENTITY AND CULTURE]**
Fourth, identity and culture. The brand places itself in music, comedy and
subculture, not in the gym or the spa.

**[2:48, the chain]**
Put together, this is how I would model it: packaging creates attention, attention
builds brand recall, and recall can turn into a purchase preference. This is a
conceptual model of the strategy, not a proven causal chain.

## 5 · The consumer behavior shift (3:00–3:50)

**[3:01, BEFORE]**
What changes for the consumer? Before: "I need water."

**[3:05, AFTER]**
After: "I need water, and I prefer a brand that reflects my personality." Notice
that the need stays exactly the same. Something is added on top of it.

**[3:14, functional → symbolic]**
The product moves from functional to symbolic.

**[3:20, the words around the can]**
In consumer-behavior terms, this is symbolic consumption: people value products
for what they signal, such as identity, humor, self-expression and cultural
association, and not only for what the product does.

**[3:40, the caveat]**
An important caveat: this is a plausible mechanism. It does not mean every
customer thinks this way, and I have no survey data showing that they do.

## 6 · What changed? (3:50–4:35)

**[3:51, container → object]**
So what actually changed? The can went from being a container to being an object
people want to be seen with.

**[3:58, the equation]**
The value equation becomes functional value, plus brand identity, plus
entertainment.

**[4:10, "THE PRODUCT REMAINS WATER…"]** *(read it slowly)*
The product remains water. The perceived meaning changes.

**[4:18, the milestones]**
And that meaning turned into a business. After the first cans in 2019, the brand
expanded into flavored sparkling water in 2022 and iced tea in 2023, the year
BeverageDaily reported 263 million dollars in retail sales. In 2024 the company
was reported to be valued at 1.4 billion dollars. These are reported trade-press
figures; I am not making market-share claims.

## 7 · Critical ethical reflection (4:35–5:40)

**[4:36, the slow fade]** *(slow down your own pace here too)*

**[4:37, question 1]**
Now the critical part. First: are consumers paying for the product, or for its
image?

**[4:43, question 2]**
Second: when does emotional persuasion become manipulation?

**[4:50, question 3]**
And third: does recyclable packaging automatically mean environmental
sustainability?

**[4:57, benefits and concerns]**
There are real benefits here: creative differentiation in a crowded market,
advertising people actually enjoy, and a distinctive non-alcoholic identity.
But there are concerns too: consumers paying a premium for symbolism, emotional
influence on purchasing decisions, and environmental claims that need independent
evidence.

**[5:15, "Infinitely recyclable"]**
Take the environmental message. "Infinitely recyclable" is the company's claim.
Whether a can is actually sustainable depends on the whole chain: collection,
recycling, manufacturing, transport and disposal.

**[5:23, the EPA figure]**
The U.S. EPA reports that in 2018 about half of aluminum beer and soft-drink cans
were recycled, 50.4 percent. ◇ Critics also point out that cans carry a thin
plastic liner, and that refilling a reusable bottle is still the greener option.

**[5:33, the conclusion line]**
So recyclable does not automatically mean sustainable. My conclusion is that
Liquid Death is not inherently unethical, but its environmental claims deserve the
same scrutiny as its jokes.

## 8 · Conclusion (5:40–6:10)

**[5:41, the can returns]**
Let me come back to where we started.

**[5:49, "MARKETING DOESN'T JUST SELL PRODUCTS."]**
Liquid Death did not create the need for water.

**[5:53–5:57]** *(hold, silence)*

**[5:57, "IT SELLS MEANING."]**
It changed what buying water means, turning an ordinary product into a symbol of
identity and rebellion.

**[6:02, the final question]**
So the question I want to leave you with is this: are we buying what a product
does, or what it represents?
