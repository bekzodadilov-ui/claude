## 1.m4a — Hook (~25 s)

This is how most of us picture bottled water: clear, clean, simple. A product we buy for one reason, which is that we are thirsty.

So why does this water look like a can of beer at a metal concert?

That is my question today: how can marketing make an ordinary product feel culturally different?

## 2.m4a — Before the brand (~35 s)

Start with the category. Before Liquid Death, the need for water was already fully established. Nobody had to be convinced that hydration matters.

Water brands met that functional need, competed on price, on where you could buy them, and on how they looked on the shelf.

So the need already existed. What brands competed for was attention. To be fair, many water brands already used distinctive marketing. Liquid Death did not invent branding in this category.

## 3.m4a — Introducing Liquid Death (~40 s)

Enter Liquid Death.

A tall aluminum can. Heavy-metal typography. A skull. Every visual cue is borrowed from places water usually never goes: beer, energy drinks, punk and metal culture.

The brand was founded by Mike Cessario, a former advertising creative, and it sold its first cans in 2019, according to BeverageDaily and TechCrunch.

Look at the contrast. Conventional water branding promises purity, nature and freshness. Liquid Death promises rebellion, humor and attitude.

My reading, and I want to flag it as interpretation: their main differentiation was not a new liquid. It was a new identity built around an old one.

## 4.m4a — How the marketing works (~55 s)

How does that identity get built? I see four mechanisms.

First, visual differentiation. On a shelf of clear bottles, a black tallboy is impossible to miss.

Second, shock and humor. "Murder your thirst" is the company's own slogan, and it is deliberately absurd for a product that is just water.

Third, entertainment-first content. The ads are made to be watched and shared: a 2022 regional Super Bowl spot where kids "chug" tallboys, ending with "Don't be scared. It's just water"; a 2020 heavy-metal album built from real hate comments; a 2022 Halloween collaboration with Martha Stewart; and a 2023 blind taste test against back sweat.

Fourth, identity and culture. The brand places itself in music, comedy and subculture, not in the gym or the spa.

Put together, this is how I would model it: packaging creates attention, attention builds brand recall, and recall can turn into a purchase preference. This is a conceptual model of the strategy, not a proven causal chain.

## 5.m4a — The consumer behavior shift (~45 s)

What changes for the consumer? Before: "I need water."

After: "I need water, and I prefer a brand that reflects my personality." Notice that the need stays exactly the same. Something is added on top of it.

The product moves from functional to symbolic.

In consumer-behavior terms, this is symbolic consumption: people value products for what they signal, such as identity, humor, self-expression and cultural association, and not only for what the product does.

An important caveat: this is a plausible mechanism. It does not mean every customer thinks this way, and I have no survey data showing that they do.

## 6.m4a — What changed? (~40 s)

So what actually changed? The can went from being a container to being an object people want to be seen with.

The value equation becomes functional value, plus brand identity, plus entertainment.

The product remains water. The perceived meaning changes.

And that meaning turned into a business. After the first cans in 2019, the brand expanded into flavored sparkling water in 2022 and iced tea in 2023, the year BeverageDaily reported 263 million dollars in retail sales. In 2024 the company was reported to be valued at 1.4 billion dollars. These are reported trade-press figures; I am not making market-share claims.

## 7.m4a — Critical ethical reflection (~60 s, read slower)

Now the critical part. First: are consumers paying for the product, or for its image?

Second: when does emotional persuasion become manipulation?

And third: does recyclable packaging automatically mean environmental sustainability?

There are real benefits here: creative differentiation in a crowded market, advertising people actually enjoy, and a distinctive non-alcoholic identity. But there are concerns too: consumers paying a premium for symbolism, emotional influence on purchasing decisions, and environmental claims that need independent evidence.

Take the environmental message. "Infinitely recyclable" is the company's claim. Whether a can is actually sustainable depends on the whole chain: collection, recycling, manufacturing, transport and disposal.

The U.S. EPA reports that in 2018 about half of aluminum beer and soft-drink cans were recycled, 50.4 percent. Critics also point out that cans carry a thin plastic liner, and that refilling a reusable bottle is still the greener option.

So recyclable does not automatically mean sustainable. My conclusion is that Liquid Death is not inherently unethical, but its environmental claims deserve the same scrutiny as its jokes.

## 8.m4a — Conclusion (~25 s)

Let me come back to where we started.

Liquid Death did not create the need for water.

It changed what buying water means, turning an ordinary product into a symbol of identity and rebellion.

So the question I want to leave you with is this: are we buying what a product does, or what it represents?
